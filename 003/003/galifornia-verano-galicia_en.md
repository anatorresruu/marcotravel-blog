# What to do in a summer in Galicia: 3 routes to choose according to your style

We are always haunted by the same cliché: that it rains all year round in Galicia, or that summer here is just a three-day mirage. But those of us who live here know the truth. When the heat kicks in, this land turns into a custom-made paradise that we might well baptize as **Galifornia**: white sand beaches that look like the Caribbean, sunsets that stay etched in your memory, and a light that holds out until ten at night. The difference is that here, instead of avocado, you have <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">pulpo á feira</span>, and instead of waves and Californian surfers, you have mussel rafts (<span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">bateas</span>) appearing in every estuary.

The problem isn't that there is a lack of plans in Galicia — the problem is that Galicia is infinite, and wanting to cover all of it in a few days is mission impossible. To keep you from going crazy trying to square an impossible map, we have prepared three routes with three totally different souls: islands and estuaries, a wild coast with lighthouses, or inland vineyards. ## Plan 1: A weekend of islands, hórreos, and the best sunset of your life

This is a way to make the most of a weekend in the Rías Baixas without complications: one island day, a second day between Pontevedra and Combarro, and a sunset in Bueu that wraps up the trip in style.

### Day 1 — Cíes or Ons
Both islands are part of the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Illas Atlánticas National Park</span>, which implies an important condition to keep in mind: between May 15 and September 15, access is limited by law, with a daily visitor quota and mandatory authorization in addition to the boat ticket. It’s not a recommendation, it’s a requirement — in the middle of August, it’s best to request authorization on the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">official website of the Xunta</span> at least a week in advance. Cíes is reached from Vigo, Cangas, or Baiona; Ons, from Bueu, Portonovo, or Sanxenxo, with a crossing of about 40 minutes and fewer crowds than its larger sister. Whatever your choice, dedicate the whole day to it: beach, trail, and a leisurely stroll, with no more plans on the agenda.

If you leave from Vigo, take the opportunity to visit <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">O Berbés</span> before catching your car. It is the seafaring neighborhood attached to the port, the city's fishing heart for centuries, which still retains that essence, with lifelong taverns where you can dine and have a glass of wine at ease. If you left from Bueu or Portonovo, a walk around the port and dinner at a local seafood restaurant fulfills exactly the same function: wrapping up the day nicely before the second leg.

### Day 2 — Pontevedra, Combarro, and sunset in Bueu
Start in Pontevedra, leisurely. Its old town is one of the best preserved in Galicia, and moving from square to square —Praza da Leña, Praza da Ferrería, Praza da Verdura— is almost a game, as each one has its own atmosphere. Along the way, look out at the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Igrexa da Peregrina</span>, with its curious scallop-shaped floor plan, one of the most recognizable images of the center. If you feel like grabbing a bite, the Praza da Verdura area brings together several traditional tapas bars. Save your real appetite for what comes next.

Continue towards <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Combarro</span> and eat there. Its raised granaries (<span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">hórreos</span>) are built right on the rocks, so close to the water line that at high tide they look like they are floating. After eating, a walk through the narrow stone streets, among stone crosses (<span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">cruceiros</span>) and fishermen's houses, is a great way to digest before heading to Bueu.

![Traditional hórreos in Combarro](https://st5.depositphotos.com/15323148/66365/i/600/depositphotos_663659642-stock-photo-fishing-village-combarro-main-attraction.jpg)

To close the day, the moment you won't want to miss: the sunset at Tuia or Portomaior. Neither beach should be enjoyed in a hurry, so arrive with time. At Portomaior, the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Carmela beach bar</span> is a safe bet, with good food facing the sea. At Tuia, more sheltered and with fewer people, <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Chiringuito Galifornia</span> is famous for its mojitos. Both offer dinner service and, on some summer nights, live concerts as the sun bids farewell over the estuary. Whatever your choice, the sunset is the best way to close the day. Nothing else is needed.

## Plan 2: The Lighthouse Route, a well-lived stretch

The <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Lighthouse Route</span> covers about 200 km along the Costa da Morte, uniting lighthouses, cliffs, and fishing villages in one of Galicia's most spectacular coastal itineraries. Doing the whole thing in a single day is not the best idea; the way to truly enjoy it on a getaway is to choose a stretch and walk it calmly.

A good starting point is the area of <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Muxía and Fisterra</span>. The <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Fisterra Lighthouse</span>, which for centuries marked what was believed to be the end of the world, and the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Sanctuary of A Barca in Muxía</span>, with the rocks that tradition associates with the legend of the Virgin, are a must-see stop on this stretch. If you prefer fewer people and equally impressive landscapes, the stretch towards <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Cabo Vilán</span> in Camariñas, with its lighthouse and cliffs, is another great option.

![Wild coastal cliffs and lighthouse Cabo Vilan](https://st5.depositphotos.com/22948272/62976/i/600/depositphotos_629762390-stock-photo-lighthouse-cabo-vilan-camarinas-galicia.jpg)

Whichever stretch you choose, do it on foot. It’s the best way to truly enjoy the landscape, and along the way, you earn the big feast that comes next.

For lunch, in Fisterra, a good option is <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Restaurante O Centolo</span>, next to the port and overlooking the sea, specializing in local fish and seafood. If your stretch was Camariñas, by the port you will find simple seafood restaurants with great daily produce. And if you fancy something different from fish, in either town you will also find places with good Galician meat and <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">pulpo á feira</span>, the area's other unmissable gastronomic date. If what you are looking for are unforgettable views, head towards <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Ézaro</span>: the Xallas river waterfall drops almost all the way to the sea, and its terraces offer panoramas worth stopping for on their own.

The key to Plan 2 is not completing the 200 km, but leaving you wanting to come back next year for the next stretch.

## Plan 3: Ribeira Sacra, the other Galician summer

If the first two plans taste like the sea, the Ribeira Sacra is the inland Galician summer: canyons carved by the Sil and Miño rivers, heroic vineyards on impossible terraces, and monasteries that have been part of the landscape for centuries.

The best way to experience the area is from the water, taking a <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">catamaran tour through the Sil Canyon</span>, which allows you to see from below what from land can only be guessed: rock walls over 500 meters high and vineyards clinging to almost vertical slopes. They depart from various docks, such as Doade or the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Belesar recreational area</span>, and it is wise to book in advance during peak season.

On land, two stops shape the day: the <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Monastery of Santo Estevo</span>, now a Parador, with its cloisters from different eras and a very characteristic silence, and a viewpoint in the area —<span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Cabezoás or Vilouxe</span> are good options— to see the riverbank from above, with the vineyards drawing lines across the canyon.

![Sil Canyon and vineyards in Ribeira Sacra](https://st5.depositphotos.com/2071605/71639/i/600/depositphotos_716395828-stock-photo-landscape-sil-river-ribeira-sacra.jpg)
And since you are in vineyard territory, it is well worth wine-tasting at a winery featuring <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">Mencía or Godello</span>, the two varieties that define Ribeira Sacra. For lunch, <span style="color: #FF7F50; text-decoration: underline;">A Faragulla</span> in the historic center of Chantada is a secure recommendation: well-treated local product cuisine in a simple, cozy atmosphere, perfect for wrapping up a plan as Galician and summery as the other two, even if it has little to do with the coast.
